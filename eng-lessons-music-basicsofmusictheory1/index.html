Learning the Basics of Music Theory — Part 1 (ver.261023)

I’ve started studying music so I can compose my own pieces.

Let me share what I learned today. I hope you find it helpful!

The piano is an instrument we’re all familiar with.

On a piano, moving up to the next adjacent key—counting both white and black keys—means going up a semitone, or half step. Moving up two such steps means going up a whole tone, or whole step.

If you look at a piano keyboard, there is no black key between E and F. Moving from E to F is still a semitone. The absence of a black key does not make it a whole tone!

Now, let’s talk about sound.

Sound comes from vibrations. When a sound source vibrates, it transfers those vibrations to the surrounding air particles. Those particles pass the vibrations to neighboring particles, which pass them to other particles, and so on…

Eventually, the vibrations reach our ears and make our eardrums vibrate. Our brains then interpret the signals as “sound.”

The sounds we hear can be visualized and described numerically. One simple way to visualize them is with a waveform graph.

When you think of a waveform, you might picture a repeating graph and a number such as 220 Hz. If that doesn’t ring a bell, that’s fine—you can start learning about it now!

[Image]

As shown here, a tone can be described using its frequency, measured in hertz (Hz). This tells us how many times something vibrates per second. In this example, 220 Hz means 220 vibrations per second.

We can represent this repeating vibration as a graph. That is a waveform graph.

The x-axis represents time, while the y-axis shows the vibration’s displacement. The amplitude describes how far the vibration moves away from its resting position—think of it as how far it swings up and down.

Generally, a larger amplitude means a louder sound. A shorter period—meaning the repeating cycles are more tightly packed along the horizontal axis—corresponds to a higher pitch.

When studying music, we often hear the terms consonance and dissonance.

Some notes blend smoothly when played together. Others produce a sound that makes us go, “Hmm? That sounds a little strange.”

A common way to find consonant combinations is to keep the ratio between the notes’ frequencies simple.

What would a very simple ratio look like?

For example, we could have ratios such as 1:2, 1:3, and so on.

In other words, we multiply a frequency by whole numbers: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5…

For example:

  • 220 × 2 = 440 Hz
  • 220 × 3 = 660 Hz
  • 220 × 4 = 880 Hz
  • …and so on.

If we collect a fundamental frequency and its positive integer multiples, we get a harmonic series.

The fundamental is the lowest, basic frequency of a sound. The higher frequencies that sound alongside it at integer multiples of that fundamental are called harmonics.

That’s it for Part 1.

See you in Part 2!
